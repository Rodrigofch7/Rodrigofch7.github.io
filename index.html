About

What I do

I'm a Master's student in Computational Analysis and Public Policy at the University of Chicago. I work with large public datasets to answer questions about cities: property tax rolls, housing sales, satellite images, hospital records, century-old archives.

Two things make up most of what I do. The first is causal inference, which means working out whether a policy actually caused a change or just happened around the same time. The second is machine learning on big datasets, usually to find patterns or flag the cases worth a closer look. I've used both on housing, transit, water systems, and policing, in Brazil, Senegal, India, and the United States.

I also read the rules. Property tax assessment, zoning, and infrastructure regulation all have detailed legal machinery behind them, and that machinery usually explains what the data is doing. Knowing both sides is what makes an analysis survive someone pushing back on it.

This summer I worked with the World Bank's DECDI team in Washington, DC. I measured how construction in Dakar responded to a new bus rapid transit line using satellite imagery, and I built a timeline of forty years of Mumbai property tax policy, then tested the assessment records for signs that declared values were being manipulated. Before that, at UChicago's Mansueto Institute, I digitised century-old insurance registers and linked them to historical censuses to map where industry sat in Chicago a hundred years ago. I go back there in September 2026 to write up the method.

I'm a committed cyclist, which is part of why I keep coming back to how cities move people and who they get built for. Portuguese is my first language, I'm fluent in English and conversational in Spanish.

Research

Publications & working papers

Private Ownership of Water and Wastewater Systems: Assessing Health Impacts

Utilities Policy, peer-reviewed journal article (first author)

In 2020 Brazil made it easier for private companies to run water and sewage systems. This paper asks whether that was a good idea, by looking at what happened in the municipalities that had already privatised. Comparing closely matched municipalities before and after they switched, between 1998 and 2021, we find fewer hospital admissions for diarrhoeal disease among children under five, and no change in diseases unrelated to sanitation. The effect is not uniform. Some municipalities improved and others did not, and the clearest evidence comes from Tocantins, the one state that moved to full private ownership. I wrote the paper, built the dataset, and wrote the code. My co-author supervised.

Resistance to Doing Right: Rising Cost of Misconduct from Body-Worn Camera Adoption

Working paper, in progress (second author)

Between 2021 and 2022 São Paulo issued 10,125 body cameras across 64 police battalions. Using confidential police records, we asked what changed. Battalion boundaries are not public, so I rebuilt them by clustering the locations of recorded incidents. Confrontations between police and civilians fell, and the drop was concentrated in low-income areas with no measurable change in wealthier ones. Property crime rose over the same period, most clearly vehicle theft. Together that looks less like better policing and more like less policing.

NYC Property Tax: A Horizontal-Equity Audit

Python · LightGBM · scikit-learn

Two houses that are alike should pay similar property tax. This checks whether that holds in New York. Comparing every property to a citywide average is meaningless when a Manhattan co-op and a Queens two-family sit in the same column, so instead each property is compared only against properties genuinely like it: same borough, building class, tax class, decade built, size, and price range. On that basis about 43% sit more than 15% away from what their closest comparables pay. How much you find depends on how you define a comparable, which was our choice, so this measures how spread out assessments are within similar groups rather than proving anyone was assessed wrongly. I designed the comparison method, built the data pipeline, and trained the models.

Data Centers Next Door

R · Shiny · Spatial Econometrics

Data centres are going up fast around Chicago, and people living nearby want to know what that does to their bills and their home values. This puts the question on a map. I scraped 124 listed facilities, confirmed 45 of them, found their locations, and worked out roughly when each opened from air permit filings. Those dates are lined up against Zillow home values and Census household and utility costs across 660 ZIP codes, so you can see what moved before and after each opening. It shows patterns, not causes: a data centre opening near rising prices does not mean it caused them. I did the scraping, the geocoding, and the dashboard.
